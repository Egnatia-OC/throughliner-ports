Research was run at the user's direction on how legislative prose is actually shaped, filed as `resources/research/legislative-prose-syntax.md`. It answers the question the earlier law research left open — what makes one rule a subset of another — and the answer is syntactic rather than topical: a unit is genuinely subordinate when at least two parallel units exist, each reads grammatically as a continuation of the parent's opening words, all share one grammatical function, every modifier points only at the opening words or its own unit, and none is a complete sentence. Most nested bullets in `plugin-behaviour.md` fail the last test, which means the file's apparent hierarchy overstates how much of it is genuinely subordinate — and under the instruction ceiling a true subordinate shares its parent's slot while a complete sentence consumes one. The user's governing principle followed: where a rule can be written as a subordinate unit, it is, and freestanding is for where it is necessary. The research also found the method taking the losing side of a live drafting dispute by inheritance — the gate instructs signalling exceptions before the general rule, Coode's 19th-century convention, where modern evidence finds front-loaded conditions measurably harder to comprehend.

It was added as an amendment to the existing wording rule rather than as a new rule, so it consumes no slot.
