# [HASH] — Four rationale paragraphs cut from the always-loaded rules, and the two anti-re-proposal guards among them preserved here

The highest-value cut on the rationale audit's list, because this file loads in every session of every command — a word removed here is a word saved everywhere, forever. Four paragraphs went: the retired-stored-mode-settings sentences beneath the show-first rule, the "what this gives up" paragraph beneath the capture-placement rule, the whole paragraph recording that the 20% index-line cap was repealed on measurement, and the evidence sentence beneath the check-our-own-conformance rule. Each states why its rule is worded as it is, and each rule reads complete without it.

The reasoning here was contested and the item said so at planning rather than pretending otherwise. Two of the four are **anti-re-proposal guards** — the 20% cap paragraph says outright that it is recorded so the cap is not restored. Cutting them moves the guard into the session record, where a session about to re-propose the cap may not look. That is a real loss, not a technicality.

It was cut anyway, and the reason is the file's own economics. The guard's value is occasional and conditional: it pays only in the rare session that considers restoring a repealed thing. The cost is unconditional and permanent: every session of every command carries those words. Against a corpus whose central problem is that every rule admitted degrades the rules already there, an occasional guard sitting in the always-loaded layer is exactly the wrong trade. Both guards' content is written into this entry in full, which is where the method routes rationale by design.

**The stored-mode guard, preserved.** The method has retired stored mode settings twice, and both times the stored field recorded something that was not stable about the user. A show-first preference is less stable still: it is about where they are right now, not who they are. That is the argument against ever writing the show-first switch to a file.

**The 20%-cap guard, preserved.** The cap was derived from three entries of 968, 1,055 and 1,738 words — all long. Measured across the whole index it proved monotonic in *entry* length rather than in line length: not one of the fifteen entries over 1,000 words breached it, while two thirds of the entries under 200 words did, the worst being a 34-word line against an 87-word entry. So it never fired on the thing it was aimed at, and fired 117 times on work nobody thinks is wrong. The distribution is in `resources/research/index-line-length-distribution.md`, which is a durable file precisely so this can be re-read word-for-word before anyone restores a figure.

The "No replacement number" paragraph that follows the repealed cap was **not** cut. It states that scoping a cap to entries above some length reintroduces a bare figure and that absolute length discriminates nothing — which is an operative prohibition on a future author, not an account of a past decision.
