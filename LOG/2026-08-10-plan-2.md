# [UNFILLED] — Twelve items processed and the rule-lifecycle design given its missing half: law's own review cycles, and the finding that a duty to review is the shape that fails

The session's largest outcome is that [rule-lifecycle-system] stopped being a list of stages and acquired a governing principle, from research the user asked for on the grounds that law is where this project already borrows its drafting rules. `resources/research/legislative-review-cycles.md` records it: a sunset clause works because inaction *repeals*, so the default state does the work; post-legislative scrutiny fails because inaction leaves everything as it was and produces no signal. Only 7.6% of 344 eligible UK Acts between 2005 and 2017 received the scrutiny they were due, and where reports did land, 61% of recommendations were rejected or ignored, with acceptance falling to zero for substantial ones. All three of this project's recorded cycle failures — the merge cycle, the corpus reaching ~218 instructions, the FAQ stale since June — are duties to review. That became Constraint D, alongside three bounding findings: scope the obligation narrowly, give maintenance a standing owner rather than the session already doing the primary work, and size findings small.

Two captures folded onto the same item rather than sitting beside it, and both were relocations rather than deletions. The prior-art argument that the whole apparatus may be unnecessary was kept but **bounded by the user's correction** — retiring one document does not put the rest of the docset on a cycle, and the need for cyclical review is evidenced three times independently of it. The FAQ was named as a covered subject, which the five stages as written would have missed entirely since all of them take a *rule* as their subject. That created a real tension the design must hold rather than trade away: widening the subject to documentation pulls against Constraint D's narrow-scope finding, and subject and scope are different axes.

**The self-authoring gate's why-clause exception became repeal-and-replace across one long exchange in which the user defeated three Claude proposals in succession**, each recorded on the item so the shapes are not re-proposed. The replacement is reclassification rather than exemption: if a reason is genuinely needed to apply a rule, it is not rationale attached to the rule but part of it, written as operative text — law's purpose clause. The mechanism is three-part and **the order is the safeguard**: admission on recorded evidence of misapplication, then protection by grammar (write it into the operative sentence so removing it leaves the rule incomplete), then review from the drafting note that cites the instance. Grammar as the *admission* test was rejected as a bloat vector — welding any sentence into the operative clause would make it permanently unstrippable — and shipping the admitting instance alongside the clause was rejected as shipping history with the rules. The instance never ships; only the clause does. Vocabulary was settled at the same time: **throughline** for why the project decided something, **drafting note** for why a rule is worded as it is, and **purpose clause** for the thing that looks like a why and is not. The user-facing name for "why the method behaves this way" is left open.

**Two rules were agreed and then deliberately blocked, on the user's instruction that no further exception-shaped rule may be admitted until exception handling exists.** The derivation rule bans a limit that does not state what it was derived from; Claude's narrowing was accepted, since the user's first form — only proportional bounds allowed — would have outlawed the research-backed 150–200 instruction ceiling and the externally-imposed 10,000-character hook cap. The index-line cap was measured rather than chosen: three real entry/index pairs sit at 13%, 17% and 19%, so the user's proposed 50% would have licensed index lines to triple, and 20% freezes current practice. Both now wait on [exception-handling-in-rule-authoring].
